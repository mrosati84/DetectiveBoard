What is a detective board?

A detective board is a wall of evidence: photos, notes and documents pinned to a cork board and linked with red string to show how they relate. Depending on who you ask it’s an investigation board, an evidence board, a suspect board, a crime board or — when the string gets out of hand — a conspiracy board.

Whatever you call it, the idea is the same. Getting every piece of information out of your head and onto one surface makes patterns visible: who knew whom, what happened when, and which clue doesn’t fit. It works just as well for a true crime case, a novel, a tabletop RPG campaign or a history assignment as it does for a fictional detective.

What you need

For a physical detective board:

  • A cork board — at least 60 × 90 cm (2 × 3 ft). Bigger is better; cases always grow.
  • Push pins — map pins with round heads hold string best.
  • Red string — red yarn, embroidery thread or baker’s twine. Anything thin and bright.
  • Index cards and sticky notes for names, facts and hunches.
  • Printed photos of people, places and objects.
  • A marker and scissors.

For an online detective board you only need a browser — DetectiveBoard is free and needs nothing more than a username and password.

How to make a physical detective board

  1. Define the question. Write the mystery you’re trying to solve on a card and pin it at the top: “Who took the key?”, “What happened on Route 9?”, “Why does chapter 14 feel wrong?”.
  2. Make a card for every person. Suspects, witnesses, victims, characters. Add a photo if you have one, and a line or two on motive or role.
  3. Add the evidence. Objects, documents, places and events each get their own card. Keep one fact per card so you can move them independently.
  4. Lay out a rough structure. People in the centre, evidence around them, or a timeline running left to right along the bottom. Leave empty space — you’ll need it.
  5. Pin everything down. Put a pin through the top of each card, leaving a few millimetres of the pin exposed so string can wrap around it.
  6. Run the red string. Tie the string to one pin, stretch it to the next and wrap it twice around that pin. Each length of string is one claim: “these two are connected”. Write why on a sticky note if it isn’t obvious.
  7. Step back and look for patterns. Cards with many strings are central. Cards with none are either irrelevant or the clue everyone missed.

How to make a detective board online

A physical board looks great on camera, but it’s hard to rearrange, impossible to take with you and the string tangles the moment you move a card. An online detective board keeps the cork, the pins and the red string, and drops the hassle.

  1. Open a free board. Sign up on DetectiveBoard with a username and password and create a new board.
  2. Add cards. Use the toolbar to add a card for each person, clue and place. Give it a title, a description and, if you like, a photo.
  3. Drop sticky notes for hunches and open questions.
  4. Connect the dots. Select two cards and tie them with red string. The yarn sags under gravity, just like the real thing — and it follows the cards when you drag them.
  5. Rearrange freely. Zoom, pan and move cards around as your theory changes. No pins to pull, no string to re-tie.
  6. Share or export. Send a read-only link so others can follow your reasoning, or export the board as PNG, PDF or SVG.

Tips for a board that actually helps

  • One question per board. If you’re chasing two mysteries, make two boards.
  • One fact per card. Combined facts can’t be connected separately.
  • Make every string mean something. If you can’t say why two cards are linked, don’t link them.
  • Write down your sources. Knowing where a fact came from tells you how much to trust it.
  • Prune regularly. Remove dead ends instead of letting them clutter the board. A tidy evidence board thinks more clearly than a messy one.
